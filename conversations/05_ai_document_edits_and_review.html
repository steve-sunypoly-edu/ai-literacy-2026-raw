Conversation Overview

[The author] (SMS) is a SUNY faculty member and AI Fellows for the Public Good participant developing an AI literacy curriculum framework. His core framework is RTW — Reading, Thinking, Writing — which defines AI literacy as practicing these skills using tools consistent with one's peers. He is building a suite of interconnected documents including the AI-FYE (AI in the First Year Experience) faculty invitation, a Learner's Permit orientation scaffold, and a Commercial Driver's License framework. [The author] works primarily through voice/audio input, which means his prompts are conversational and sometimes incomplete mid-thought; Claude is expected to track context carefully and reconstruct intent from fragments.

The conversation had two phases. First, [The author] shared the full AI-FYE HTML document and the [Colleague] thread — a rich working conversation with a writing professor who challenged [The author]'s definition of writing-as-prompting. That thread produced three deliverables (reply to [Colleague], reflective memo, revision directions) and several key formulations [The author] wants preserved: "the transcript is the draft pile and the thinking record and the evidence of authorship all at once"; "the artifact without the transcript is like grading a painting without knowing if the student held the brush"; and the hypertext-node framing ("each prompt is a node, the conversation is the traversal, the artifact is one path's endpoint, not the map"). [The author] explicitly flagged the node/traversal formulation as worth developing as a theoretical anchor for transcript assessment, connected to his MICA framework and monadological approach.

Second, [The author] and Claude worked through substantive revisions to the AI-FYE document itself, informed by the [Colleague] thread, [Colleague]'s critique (that AI-generated text severs the reader-author trust relationship, particularly in freshman composition contexts), and [The author]'s "AI for all people, activities, spaces, and places" foundational framework. Key decisions reached: the AI Salon should be reframed around "double vision" — students see their own work as both human-authored and machine-interpreted; the four coordinated courses are all labeled 188 and share an integrated calendar, scope, and workflow (not just timing); the Salon is AI-for-all-activities by design; assignments across participating courses should span a spectrum from fully AI-assisted to fully human-authored; and the "What This Is" section should lead with the meta-point that the document itself is a generated artifact. Three revised sections were finalized — What This Is / Introduction / Overview, The Course Structure, and Orientation Day — and [The author] requested the full HTML document with version stamps appended to each section for handoff to Claude Code. [The author]'s strong preference is to avoid AI-tell markers (smart quotes, certain phrasings) because they signal AI generation and cause readers to devalue the work. He also corrected "[Colleague] Gavin" to "[Colleague]" mid-conversation. Key colleagues mentioned: [Colleague] (writing professor, intellectual interlocutor on RTW and prompting theory) and [Colleague] (whose talk on struggle and the reader-author relationship is pending transcript and will anchor the pedagogical framing).
